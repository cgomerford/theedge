§ Pitch Grades

How a team's analytics department grades a pitch before the results come in. This page shows what each pitch does, where it goes, and how those two things combine into one grade on a 100 scale.

§ 01

Grade card

Three numbers per pitcher. Each sits on a 70 to 130 scale with 100 marked: right of the line is above average, left is below.

How to read the scale. 100 is league average. Every 10 points is one standard deviation, so 110 is clearly good and 120 is rare. Grades are defense-neutral: a ball in play is graded by how it was hit, not by who happened to field it. Leverage is not part of the grade: a pitch in a blowout counts the same as one in a tight game.
§ 02

Arsenal

One row per pitch type. Click a header to sort; click a row to choose that pitch for the next three sections. Grade cells are shaded by their distance from 100.

Choose a row. Velo in mph, IVB and HB in inches, spin in rpm.

Why it grades this way

The six things a pitch can do for a pitcher, each on the same 100 scale for the selected pitch.

§ 04

How a pitch gets graded

Every pitch ends in one of a few outcomes. The percentages on the branches show how often the selected pitch takes each path.

Each outcome at the bottom is priced by its average effect on runs across the whole league (shown as runs for the batting team: a ball adds a little, a strikeout takes some away). A pitch's grade adds up those prices, weighted by how often the pitch takes each path.

§ 05

Movement

Each solid dot is one of his pitch types, sized by how often he throws it. The faint dot is the league average for that pitch type.

Horizontal is inches toward the pitcher's arm side (right) or glove side (left); vertical is inches of rise beyond what gravity alone would give (negative means more drop than average).

§ 06

Stuff vs results

For each pitch type: what the stuff and command say should have happened (expected), next to what did happen (actual), in runs saved per 100 pitches.

When results trail the stuff, it can be sequencing, defense or noise. The stuff is the stickier signal.

§ 07

Pressure profile

High pressure means the 7th inning or later, the score within 2 runs, and a runner on base. Everything else is normal. This is a description of what happened, not a grade of nerve.

§ 09

How we grade a pitch

Outcome tree

Every pitch is followed down a tree (take or swing, then strike, ball, miss, foul or ball in play) and each ending is priced by its average effect on runs.

The 100 scale

100 is league average and every 10 points is one standard deviation. Most pitches land between 80 and 125.

Defense-neutral

Balls in play are graded by how they were hit (speed and angle), not by whether a fielder made the play.

The count

The count is accounted for in Command and Overall only: Stuff is the same in any count, while a pitch placed for the count earns Command credit.

Leverage

Leverage is not part of any grade. Game situation is shown separately in the pressure profile.

Sample size

Stuff is reliable early, within a few hundred pitches. Command needs about 400 pitches before it settles, and rows below that carry a note.
